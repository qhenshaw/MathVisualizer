Vectors · 01 · Directions

To aim at the enemy, subtract the player

The player stands at P and the enemy at Q. Q − P gives the offset d (dashed): its length is how far away the enemy is. Dividing d by its length gives the direction (green arrow): a unit vector, always length 1, that only says which way to face. Multiply it by a speed and you get movement toward the enemy that does not depend on how far away they are.

d = Q − P = (Qx − Px, Qy − Py) |d| = √(dx² + dy²) dir = d / |d| |dir| = 1 θ = atan2(dy, dx)

Vectors · 02 · Normalization

Keep the direction, throw away the length

Dividing a vector by its own length shrinks or stretches it onto the unit circle. The result n is a unit vector: length exactly 1, pointing the same way.

|v| = √(vx² + vy²) n = v / |v| = (vx / |v|, vy / |v|) |n| = 1

The zero vector has no direction, so it cannot be normalized. Real code checks |v| against a small epsilon before dividing.

Used for movement at constant speed regardless of input length, surface normals for lighting, and any formula that expects pure direction.

Vectors · 03 · Dot product

How much do two vectors agree?

The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them up. Here A and B are normalized (length 1, on the dashed unit circle), so the dot product is exactly cos θ: the length of B’s shadow on A, where the dashed line meets A’s line. The cos θ bar in this panel shows the result, green when positive and pink when negative. Positive means they point the same way, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposed.

Vectors · 04 · Cross product

A new vector, perpendicular to both

In 3D, A × B points straight out of the plane that A and B span, following the hand rule of your coordinate system. Its length equals the area of the parallelogram they make, so it shrinks to zero when A and B line up. Order matters: B × A flips it. With unit vectors A and B, the length is simply sin θ. Use the switch on the graph to flip between a left-handed system (Unity, Unreal, DirectX) and a right-handed one (OpenGL, Blender, Godot): the numbers are identical, but the y axis is drawn the other way, so A × B points out of the other side of the plane.

A × B = (AyBz − AzBy, AzBx − AxBz, AxBy − AyBx) |A × B| = |A| |B| sin θ |A| = |B| = 1 → |A × B| = sin θ

A and B are normalized (length 1). Drag their tips to turn them around the floor; use the sliders to tilt them up or down.

Vectors · 05 · Lerp

Linear interpolation: a slider between two values

Lerp blends A into B by a fraction t. At t = 0 you get A, at t = 1 you get B, and everything in between lies on the straight line joining them. It works on numbers, points and colors alike. The small dots mark t = 0, 0.1, … 1; switch on smoothstep to see easing bunch them up at the ends.

lerp(A, B, t) = A + (B − A) · t = (1 − t) · A + t · B

Rotations · 03 · Slerp

Spherical lerp: travel along the arc, not the chord

Lerping two unit vectors cuts straight across the circle (grey chord), so the result gets shorter in the middle. Slerp instead rotates at a constant angular rate along the shortest arc and stays length 1 the whole way. The pink ticks mark t = 0.1, 0.2, … and sit evenly spaced along the arc.

Ω = acos(A · B) slerp(A, B, t) = sin((1 − t)Ω) / sin Ω · A + sin(tΩ) / sin Ω · B

Rotations · 02 · Gimbal lock

Why three angles are not enough

Euler angles rotate in a fixed order: yaw around the world’s up axis, then pitch, then roll around the object’s own nose. Each ring shows one of those rotations. Pitch the nose straight up to 90° and the pink roll ring swings into the same plane as the amber yaw ring. Yaw and roll now spin the object around the same axis, and one direction of rotation is gone until you pitch back down.

R = Rz(yaw) · Ry(pitch) · Rx(roll) roll axis = R · (1, 0, 0) pitch = ±90° → roll axis ∥ yaw axis

Why it matters cameras clamp pitch to ±89°, interpolating Euler angles near the lock flips wildly, and it is the main reason engines store orientation as quaternions.

Rotations · 01 · Quaternions

A rotation packed into four numbers

A unit quaternion stores an axis u and an angle θ: the vector part is the axis scaled by sin(θ/2), the scalar part is cos(θ/2). It rotates the cube around the amber axis with no gimbal lock, composes by multiplication and slerps smoothly. Notice the half angle: after one full turn q has become −q, which is the same orientation. It takes 720° to get back to the same numbers.

q = (w, x, y, z) = (cos θ⁄2, u · sin θ⁄2) v′ = q v q⁻¹ |q| = 1

Waves & noise · 01 · Sine & cosine

The circle, unrolled

Spin a point around a circle: its height is sin θ (pink) and its sideways position is cos θ (blue). Plot the height over time and you get a sine wave; cosine is the very same wave shifted a quarter turn. Amplitude scales the height, frequency sets how fast it spins, phase sets where it starts.

y = A · sin(2π f t + φ) cos θ = sin(θ + 90°) sin²θ + cos²θ = 1

Waves & noise · 02 · Sawtooth

A ramp that keeps resetting

Take a steadily increasing value and keep only its fractional part: it climbs from 0 to 1, snaps back, and repeats. That is a sawtooth. Fold it and you get a triangle wave; threshold it and you get a square wave. The bar on the right is the output driving something.

Waves & noise · 03 · Perlin noise

Smooth randomness from a grid of arrows

Perlin noise puts a random gradient arrow on every grid corner. For any point, the four corners around it each vote with the dot product of their arrow and the offset to the point (amber numbers), and the votes are blended with a smooth fade curve. The result is random but continuous. Stack octaves, each twice the frequency and half the strength, for fractal detail. Drag the probe; the strip below is a slice along its row.

Physics · 01 · Acceleration & impulse

Forces change velocity; impulses change it instantly

A constant force F gives the cart a constant acceleration a = F / m, so its velocity changes at a steady rate: a straight slope on the graph. An impulse J is a force packed into a single instant, so velocity jumps by J / m with no slope at all. When the cart hits a wall, the wall applies an impulse of its own: a light bounce that keeps only 30% of the speed, so a cart pushed into a wall soon comes to rest against it. Heavier carts respond less to both.

a = F / m v ← v + a · Δt x ← x + v · Δt impulse: v ← v + J / m wall bounce: v ← −0.3 · v

Used for gravity and thrusters (AddForce every frame), jumps, explosions, hits and knockback (one-off impulses), and character controllers.

Physics · 02 · Linear & angular drag

Everything slows down by a fraction per second

Drag removes a fixed fraction of speed every second, so motion fades out smoothly instead of stopping dead: an exponential decay. Linear drag slows sliding (the puck), angular drag slows spinning (the wheel); they work the same way on different quantities. The graph shows both speeds falling away. Higher drag, faster fade.

v ← v · e^(−k · Δt) ≈ v − k · v · Δt (per frame) ω ← ω · e^(−k_ang · Δt) half-life = ln 2 / k

Used for air resistance and rolling friction, space-ship coasting, settling a spinning object, and smoothing player input. (In Unity these are Rigidbody drag and angular drag.)

Physics · 03 · Springs

Pulled back toward rest, harder the further you go

A spring pushes back in proportion to how far it is stretched (Hooke’s law), so the mass overshoots and oscillates. Damping removes energy in proportion to velocity, shrinking the swings. The damping ratio ζ decides the character: below 1 it bounces, exactly 1 (critical) it returns as fast as possible without overshooting, above 1 it creeps back slowly. Drag the mass and let go.

F = −k · x − c · v a = F / m ω₀ = √(k / m), T = 2π / ω₀ ζ = c / (2 √(k · m))
